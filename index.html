What it taught me

  • Design intent vs. friction. GTA V's modern control scheme and vehicle variety make it faster and more accessible, while RDR2's slower, more deliberate systems — interacting with people, switching between actions — are a deliberate choice in service of immersion, not bugs. Recognizing that distinction is exactly the nuance a tester needs before flagging a design decision as a bug.
  • Side content that makes a world feel alive. RDR2's serial killer mystery, cave drawings, UFO events, and random encounters reward exploration — side content can be evaluated for whether it makes the world feel alive, not just whether it's technically functional.

Two writing approaches

  • GTA V — Michael, Franklin, and Trevor succeed through a chaotic, comedic ensemble.

06 — Cover Letter

Why I'm applying for Game Tester at Rockstar India

Dear Hiring Team,

Game Tester · QA · Rockstar India

I'm writing to apply for the Game Tester position with Rockstar India's QA team. Gaming has been a constant part of my life since childhood, and Rockstar's catalog specifically has shaped how I think about game design, so this role is one I've genuinely been working toward.

Over the years I've played 700+ games spanning nearly every major genre — action, action-adventure, open-world, RPG, shooter, survival horror, stealth, racing, strategy, and simulation, among others — including God of War, The Witcher 3, Elden Ring, Metal Gear Solid, Resident Evil, and Mafia. That range matters for QA work: it means I judge a game against the standards of its own genre rather than a single fixed yardstick — the same discipline testers need when deciding whether a mechanic is working as intended or actually broken.

My personal favorites — Silent Hill 2 (2001), Shadow of the Colossus, Signalis, and Cuphead — tend to be games built around a very specific, deliberately controlled experience, which is probably why I notice quickly when something in a game's atmosphere, pacing, or presentation breaks that control.

My knowledge of Rockstar's recent titles specifically runs deep. I've logged 100+ hours in Grand Theft Auto V and 70+ hours in Red Dead Redemption II (PSN ID: naveed_lmaoooo), and I recently put together a full side-by-side breakdown of the two games across gameplay, story, graphics, music, multiplayer, transportation, side content, characters, and cultural impact. That comparison showed me that GTA V's modern control scheme and vehicle variety make it faster and more accessible, while RDR2's slower, more deliberate systems, like interacting with people or switching between actions, are a deliberate design choice in service of immersion rather than friction — and recognizing that distinction is exactly the kind of nuance a tester needs before flagging a design decision as a bug.

It also showed me how RDR2's world rewards exploration — the serial killer mystery, cave drawings, UFO events, and random encounters — which highlights how side content can be evaluated for whether it makes the world feel alive, not just whether it's technically functional. And it showed how GTA V's ensemble of Michael, Franklin, and Trevor and RDR2's Van der Linde gang succeed through completely different writing approaches: one chaotic and comedic, one slow-building and emotional. That's useful context when testing narrative-heavy sequences or scripted events for tonal consistency.

I scored the two games against each other honestly rather than defaulting to uniform praise: GTA V takes the edge on gameplay, multiplayer, transportation, and cultural reach, while RDR2 takes it on story, characters, graphics, and exploration. I don't think either game benefits from being rated as simply "better," and I don't think Rockstar's QA process does either. That's the same critical, category-by-category approach I'd bring to testing.

I'd welcome the chance to bring that same close, critical attention to Rockstar's next release. Thank you for your time and consideration.

— Yadiki Naveed Ahammed
